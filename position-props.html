Position guide · Front Five

1 · 3 Props

Front Five · Loosehead (1) & Tighthead (3)

← All positionsHooker →

Your three things

Start here. If you can do these three, you are doing your job.

  1. Hips below shoulders on every scrum hit, and keep your body position all the way through.
  2. Arrive at every ruck knowing your job, then use a strong body position to hold back opposition players.
  3. Know your three pod options before the ball arrives: carry, tip pass, or pull-back to the base.

Props set the platform everything else is built on. When you go forward, the whole team has more time and more space. When you go backwards, nobody does. You play a big part in the impression the referee has of our scrum strength and legality. What makes a great prop – Scrummaging excellence, mobility and ball skills, set piece reliability, physically able to endure high tempo. Versatility is critical – being able to cover both sides of the front row – loose and tighthead.

New at U14. You're in a full 8-man contested scrum for the first time. Hip height relative to shoulder height is the critical technical focus — and your flanker covers your outside hip in the scrum, but the bind and the drive are yours to own.

What you do

Open each one when you are ready. You do not need to learn it all at once.

Attack when we are set up (AMBER and GREEN)
  • After the set piece and starter play, you join whichever pod is on your side. The pod is a 3-man unit forming a diamond with a back (10 or 12) at the base.
  • Your core pod skills: carry hard into contact, execute a small tip pass left or right to your support runner, or pull the ball back to the back at your base. Know all three before the ball arrives — the defence will tell you which one is on.
  • Ruck speed is critical. The target is 3 seconds from ball-on-ground to ball-in-hand for the 9. That demands you are in close support of the carrier before contact, and technically clean at the breakdown — arriving on your feet, clearing legally, not giving away penalties.
Attack when the defence breaks open

In the GREEN zone, shorter carries and faster recycling are the priority — the gain line matters more than metres gained. If the defence is broken or disorganised, your job changes immediately — stop carrying to rucks and become a support runner. Get outside the ball carrier, stay on their shoulder, call for the offload. Chaos in the opposition is an opportunity — don't wait for the structure to reassemble.

Kicking and kick chase

Chase every kick. Your primary kick-chase role is to create pressure on the receiver and stay linked with your defensive line — not to make the tackle. Stop them having time to counter-attack by removing space and time. Fan out from inside to outside as you chase.

Defence, zone by zone

In the RED zone, you are the defensive wall — guard/post at every ruck, inside channel locked. In AMBER, arrive at every breakdown before the opposition can clear. In GREEN, stay tight to the ruck after the set piece. At every ruck, maul, and set piece — GET SOMETHING. Possession back, time won, pressure on the 9 and 10, forcing them to commit extra players to secure the ball. A competitive breakdown is never wasted even if you don't win it outright.

Stretch: where you are building towards (Outstanding)

This takes time, and that is the point. Nobody is expected to be here yet.

  • Your scrum technique puts the opposition prop under consistent pressure — legally. Sets good pictures for the referee at scrum time.
  • Mobile across the full width of the field after the ball is won — not just a tight-channel carrier.
  • Adapts scrum strategy during the match: hold, wheel, or reset — communicates it to the front row.
  • Consistently arrives at breakdowns before the opposite number and changes the defensive picture.
  • Sets the standard for effort in the forward unit through actions, not words.
Pathway lens: what pathway coaches may look for

The coaching team's understanding of what development-pathway coaches (Bath Rugby DPP) look at, at a high level. It is a guide, not a promise or a checklist.

  • Technical consistency under fatigue — does your body position hold in the 50th minute the way it does in the 5th?
  • Coachability — do you apply a correction in the same session you receive it?
  • Breakdown work rate — scouts track ruck arrivals and tackle count, not just scrum outcomes.
  • Self-awareness — do you know your specific technical weakness and are you working on it?
