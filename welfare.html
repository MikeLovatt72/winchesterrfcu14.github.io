Looking After Yourself

Six short guides. Read the quick version, then open "Go deeper" if you want the why.

  2. Read the quick version. It takes a minute.
  3. Open "Go deeper" if you want to know why it matters.

1 Getting ready to play and train

Good preparation means you walk onto the pitch calm, warm, fuelled and knowing your job.

The quick version

  • Know the plan: when, where, what the focus is and what to bring.
  • Pack your bag the night before. Don't leave it to the morning.
  • Eat and drink properly in the hours before (see Food and drink).
  • Arrive with time to warm up properly. Don't arrive and run straight on.
  • Look at your position page and pick the one thing you will focus on.
  • Tell a coach before the session if something hurts.
Go deeper: what good preparation looks like and why

The night before

Pack boots, gumshield, a full water bottle, a warm layer, spare socks and a towel or shower kit. Check the time and place. Have your evening meal and get to bed on time. Everything you sort out tonight is one less thing to think about when you are nervous or in a rush tomorrow.

On the day

Eat breakfast, and have a proper meal two to three hours before you play. Sip water through the day. Arrive in time to warm up properly with the group, not on your own in a hurry. A good warm-up raises your heart rate, gets your joints moving and gets your brain switched on, so you start the game ready rather than catching up.

Prepare your head as well as your body

Open your position page and read your "three things". Choose one to focus on today, and make it about what you do, not the score ("tell the 9 the play before the ball arrives", not "score a try"). Players who know their job before the game make better decisions, because they have already made the decision. That is why we say nobody should be deciding something for the first time on the pitch.

After the session

Take two minutes afterwards. What went well? What is one thing to work on next time? Tell a coach if you want help with it. Small reviews like this are how players improve over a season.

Quick checklist

  • Boots and gumshield
  • Full water bottle
  • Warm layer for the touchline
  • Spare socks and towel
  • Something to eat afterwards
  • Know the time, the place and the focus

2 Building a routine you can repeat

A routine is the same few steps, in the same order, every time. It makes you calm and consistent, and you stop wasting energy on worry.

The quick version

  • Start small: pick two or three steps, not ten.
  • Do them in the same order before every session and every match.
  • Include a reset for mistakes: breathe, one word, next job.
  • Make it yours. It does not have to look like anyone else's.
  • Keep it going for a few weeks before you judge whether it helps.
Go deeper: how to build yours, and why it works

Why routines help

When you do the same things before every game, your body and mind recognise the pattern and settle. You stop wondering whether you are ready, and your attention is free for the game. Consistent players are often not the most talented. They are the ones who show up in the same state every week.

A simple template

  1. Night before: pack the bag, eat well, bed on time.
  2. Morning: breakfast and water. Read your three things.
  3. Two to three hours before: proper meal.
  4. Arrival: bag down, boots on, join the group.
  5. Warm-up: the same sequence every time, with the team.
  6. Just before the start: a few slow breaths and one focus word, such as "talk" or "width".

Your reset routine

Everyone makes mistakes: a dropped ball, a missed tackle, a bad throw. A good reset takes about five seconds: one slow breath out, a word that brings you back ("next"), then your next job. Players who reset quickly are the ones coaches trust, because the next play is not spoiled by the last one.

Making it stick

Write your routine in your phone notes. Change one thing at a time. If a step doesn't help, drop it. If it does, keep it. Tell a coach what you are trying, and we will help you build on it.

3 Good sleep

Sleep is when your body repairs and grows and your brain stores what you practised. Most boys your age do best on 8 to 10 hours a night.

The quick version

  • Aim for 8 to 10 hours. Most 13 to 18 year olds do best in that range.
  • Go to bed and get up at roughly the same time, including weekends.
  • Put your phone and screens away before bed. Charge the phone outside your room if you can.
  • Keep your room cool, dark and quiet.
  • Avoid energy drinks and caffeine, especially in the afternoon and evening.
  • Sleep well across the whole week, not just the night before a match.
Go deeper: what good sleep looks like and why it matters

How much

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 8 to 10 hours in every 24 for 13 to 18 year olds, and UK health services give the same range. Every teenager is a bit different, but if you are regularly well below that, you are probably tired.

Why it matters for rugby

  • Growing and repairing. Your body does a lot of its growing and muscle repair while you sleep. Cut it short and you get less of that.
  • Learning. Sleep helps you remember the skills and plays you practised. A good night after training helps it stick.
  • Decisions and reactions. Tired players make slower decisions and more mistakes, and this is exactly the part of rugby we are working on.
  • Mood and focus. Poor sleep makes it harder to manage your emotions and concentrate, in school and on the pitch.

What helps

  • A wind-down of 30 to 60 minutes with no screens: shower, read, music, or talk.
  • Natural light in the day, and some exercise, make it easier to fall asleep at night.
  • If you wake up and can't get back to sleep, stay calm and rest. Worrying about it makes it worse.
  • One bad night is not a disaster. It is the pattern over weeks that counts.

If you are regularly struggling to sleep, tell a parent or talk to your GP. It is common and it is fixable.

4 Food and drink

Eat regular, varied meals that include carbohydrate, protein and fruit or veg, and drink water all day. You do not need a special diet.

The quick version

  • Eat regular meals. Don't skip breakfast, and don't go hungry before training.
  • Each meal: some carbohydrate for energy, some protein for repair, and fruit or veg.
  • Meal two to three hours before you play. A small snack (banana, toast) 30 to 60 minutes before if you are hungry.
  • Something to eat soon after training or a match.
  • Drink water through the day, not just at training. Pale yellow urine is a good sign.
  • No supplements, powders or energy drinks. A normal balanced diet covers it.
Go deeper: healthy approaches to food and hydration, and why

Think of food as fuel and building material

  • Carbohydrate (pasta, rice, bread, potatoes, oats, fruit) is your main fuel for running, tackling and thinking. If you do not eat enough of it, you get tired and your legs feel heavy.
  • Protein (eggs, chicken, fish, milk, yoghurt, cheese, beans, lentils) helps your muscles repair and grow. Most boys get enough from normal meals.
  • Fruit, vegetables and healthy fats (nuts, oily fish, avocado) supply vitamins and minerals, and keep you healthy and ready to train.

Timing, in plain terms

Before: a proper meal two to three hours before. If you are hungry closer to the start, a light snack such as a banana, toast or a yoghurt. Don't try anything new on match day.

After: eat a meal or snack with carbohydrate and protein reasonably soon after, such as a sandwich, milk and a banana, or pasta with chicken. This starts the repair and gets you ready for the next session.

Why hydration matters

Your body is mostly water, and even mild dehydration can leave you tired and sluggish, give you a headache and make it harder to concentrate and make decisions. Sip regularly through the day. Do not wait until you feel thirsty. Water is enough for most sessions, and sports drinks are not needed for a normal training session. A simple check is the colour of your urine: pale yellow is good, dark means drink more.

What to avoid

  • Fad diets and cutting foods out. Growing boys need plenty of food. If you are ever thinking about changing your weight or shape, talk to a parent, a coach or your GP first.
  • Supplements and energy drinks. Not needed at your age, and energy drinks are not recommended for under-16s.
  • Calling foods "good" and "bad". Variety and regular meals matter most. Treats are fine.

Easy ideas

  • Breakfast: porridge with fruit, eggs on toast, cereal with milk and a banana.
  • Before training: toast with peanut butter, a banana, a yoghurt, a cereal bar.
  • After: a sandwich, pasta and chicken, rice and veg with fish or beans, milk, a smoothie.

Parents: keep the focus on energy, health and enjoyment, not weight or appearance. If you are worried about your son's eating, speak to your GP. The UK charity Beat (beateatingdisorders.org.uk) also offers support.

5 Recovery

You get fitter and stronger during recovery, not during the session itself. Training is the push, and rest is when the body builds.

The quick version

  • Cool down: easy jog and gentle stretches after training and matches.
  • Drink and eat soon after, then wash and get warm.
  • Sleep (see Good sleep). It is the best recovery there is.
  • Easy movement the next day, such as a walk, a swim or a bike ride, if you feel stiff.
  • Keep at least one full rest day in the week.
  • A bit of soreness is normal. Sharp or lasting pain is not, so tell us.
Go deeper: what good recovery looks like and why

Why recovery is part of training

Training puts a stress on your body. When you rest, eat and sleep, the body repairs itself and comes back a little stronger. If you never give it that time, the repair does not finish: you feel flat, you get niggles, and the risk of injury goes up. That matters more at your age, because your body is also growing.

After a session or match

  1. Walk or jog easily for a few minutes to bring your heart rate down.
  2. Gentle stretches, held steadily, for the big muscles you used.
  3. Drink water and eat something with carbohydrate and protein.
  4. Get warm and changed. Don't sit around in wet kit.

The days in between

  • Light activity helps stiff legs more than lying on the sofa all day.
  • If you play several sports or train for several teams, make sure at least one day a week is properly off.
  • No gadgets or special treatments are needed. Sleep, food, water and rest do the job.

Know the difference

Normal: tired legs, general muscle soreness that eases over a day or two. Tell us: pain in one spot that is sharp, gets worse, comes back every session, causes limping, or pain around the knee, heel or hip that keeps returning (common in growing boys). Never play through it, and never worry that telling us will cost you game time.

Recovery is mental too

Rugby is a great part of life, but it should not be all of it. Time with friends, other hobbies and downtime help you come back keen. If rugby is starting to feel like a chore or a worry, tell a coach.

Head injuries are different. Any suspected concussion means off straight away and no return that day. See the Players and Parents section on the home page.

6 Stretching and yoga

Ten minutes of regular stretching or yoga can help you stay flexible, balanced and in control of your body through a long season.

The quick version

  • Before playing: moving stretches (leg swings, lunges, skips), not long holds.
  • After playing: gentle held stretches, about 30 seconds each, breathing slowly.
  • Once or twice a week at home: a short yoga or mobility routine, around 10 minutes.
  • Focus on hips, hamstrings, shoulders and ankles, and add some balance work.
  • Never push into pain. Stretching should feel like a gentle pull, not a sharp pain.
  • It is a bonus on top of strength work and the warm-up, not a replacement.
Go deeper: why it helps rugby players, and what the evidence says

What it can do for you

  • Keeps you flexible through the season. In small studies of rugby players, those doing yoga twice a week held on to their hamstring flexibility over a season, while those who didn't lost some of theirs.
  • Better balance and body control. One 12-week study found yoga reduced body sway in some balance tests for rugby players. Good balance helps with tackling, changing direction and staying on your feet at the breakdown.
  • Calm and focus. Slow breathing is a useful tool for nerves and for resetting after a mistake (see Building a routine).
  • Variety and enjoyment. It is a different way to train, and many boys like doing it with friends.

Being honest about the evidence: the studies are small and mostly on players aged 19 or older. Yoga helped players keep flexibility and balance, but there is no clear proof it makes you faster or stops injuries on its own. Treat it as one helpful tool alongside good strength work, sleep and the RFU Activate warm-up. Ask a coach about Activate.

When to stretch

Before: use moving (dynamic) stretches as part of the warm-up. Long held stretches right before sprinting or power work can briefly reduce power, so save them for afterwards. After: held stretches while the muscles are warm. Other days: a short mobility or yoga session.

A simple 10-minute routine

  1. Cat-cow on hands and knees, slowly, for about a minute.
  2. Low lunge to open the front of the hip, about 30 seconds each side.
  3. Hamstring stretch with one leg out in front, about 30 seconds each side.
  4. Figure-four stretch lying on your back, for the hips and glutes, about 30 seconds each side.
  5. Thread the needle on hands and knees, for the shoulders and upper back, about 30 seconds each side.
  6. Single-leg balance, 30 seconds each side. Make it harder by closing your eyes (stand near a wall).
  7. Child's pose with slow breaths for the last minute.

Move slowly, breathe, and stop if anything hurts. If you have an injury or a growing pain, check with a coach or your GP first.

When to talk to someone

⚠
If in doubt, sit them out. Any suspected head injury: off straight away, no return that day. Tell a coach about any knock to the head, even a minor one.
