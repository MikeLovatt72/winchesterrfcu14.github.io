13 Outside Centre

Centres · Space reader · Edge exploiter · Outside back organiser

← Inside centreFull-back →

Your three things

Start here. If you can do these three, you are doing your job.

  1. Receive at pace and attack the space outside the last inside defender.
  2. Read the last defender: fix and pass, or take the gap.
  3. Mark one opponent, do not overrun into the wing's space, and tell the wing where the defence is.

You turn the 12's work into tries. When the 12 fixes the inside defender, you exploit the space. Reading the last defender — blitzing or holding? — is the most important split-second decision in the back line. Intelligence, speed, jackalling – the outside centre is a hybrid player who can do everything an openside can do defensively and attack space created by our 12. Takes running angles that forces defenses to turn and once they have turned- you can gain yards and release wingers.

New at U14. The larger pitch means more space on your outside edge than ever before. Defensive drift becomes more important — marking one man, not overrunning into the wing's territory. Counter-attack from kick receipt is now a real option.

What you do

Open each one when you are ready. You do not need to learn it all at once.

Attack when we are set up (AMBER and GREEN)
  • You are the outer decision-maker in the backs. When the 12 carries and commits defenders, the ball should reach you with one or fewer defenders to beat before the wing. Your read — fix the last inside defender and pass, or take the gap yourself — is the most important decision in the back line at that moment.
  • You can also be the back who plugs into the wide channel diamond alongside the blindside flanker or hooker — giving the wide "1" forward a distribution option from depth.
  • In the GREEN zone, if you receive the ball with space outside, the pass to the wing must be immediate — do not wait for contact.
Attack when the defence breaks open

When the defence breaks, you are the read-and-react player. If the 12 has gone and there is space, get on their outside shoulder and call. If the wing is in space and you have the ball, release immediately — don't carry into coverage when the overlap is there. The outside edge in chaos is the most dangerous attacking option on the pitch. Look to make passes and loop behind your receiver, making yourself an outside option.

Kicking and kick chase

You rarely kick in attack — but in the Green zone, if the 10 calls for a kick and you are nearest, a grubber or chip in the right direction can be the correct call. More importantly, you are a kick-chase option — when the 10 or 9 kicks from depth, your angle of chase from the mid-field position closes off the counter-attack channel inside the wings.

Defence, zone by zone

Your channel is the outside edge on the 15 metre channel — mark the opposition 13 or whoever enters your channel. Drift discipline: mark one opponent, do not overrun into the wing's territory. Communicate the wing's position in real time. RED zone: a missed tackle in the outside channel is a try conceded — stay disciplined, don't fly off your feet. GREEN zone: your organisation of the outside backs is as important as your individual tackle. At every defensive ruck — your line speed off the mark forces their decision faster. An organised, flat defensive line from 12 to 13 to wing is the most direct way to put their 10 under pressure. That pressure is the return — even without a turnover.

Stretch: where you are building towards (Outstanding)

This takes time, and that is the point. Nobody is expected to be here yet.

  • Every time you receive the ball the defence is under immediate pressure — you make the right call at pace.
  • Creates tries for the wing from situations where most players would be stopped.
  • Organises the outside back line — your communication makes wing and fullback more effective.
  • Counter-attack from deep — reads the kick and joins the back three to exploit it.
  • Carries and offloads simultaneously — the ball goes forward and so does the next phase. Dynamically attacks angles and shoulders forcing defenders to expose soft shoulders or turn away from their defensive partners.
Pathway lens: what pathway coaches may look for

The coaching team's understanding of what development-pathway coaches (Bath Rugby DPP) look at, at a high level. It is a guide, not a promise or a checklist.

  • Reading the last defender — the most sophisticated skill Bath assess in centres. Can you describe what you saw and why you chose it?
  • Defensive drift — do you maintain your channel under pressure across a full match?
  • Communication with the wing — is it real-time and accurate, or absent? Knowing when to hold the defence in place or push/drift to the outside.
  • Scoring involvement — tries scored or created over a season. Bath track this.
